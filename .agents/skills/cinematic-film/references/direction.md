# Direction: how to have the idea, defend it, and know it is right

The craft references in this folder tell you how to move a camera, ease a spring and master a
score. This one is about the part that decides whether any of that matters: the idea, the
mechanic that carries it, and the judgement that keeps it from sliding back to the average.

The public sources for everything below are in [sources.md](../sources.md), under "Slop and
novelty". Where a rule is judgement rather than finding, it says so.

---

## 1. Slop is a position, not a tool

A model sampling with no direction returns the safe centre of its training data. Anthropic's own
name for it is distributional convergence: Inter, purple gradients on white, centred hero, three
cards, a stagger on everything. A designer under deadline does the same thing and calls it a
first idea.

So slop is not "made by AI". A code-drawn noise blob with bloom, an orbit camera left running,
default chrome PBR, stock springs that all overshoot: that is the AI look with no AI involved.
And any published "use these instead" list becomes the next centre. Anthropic recommended Space
Grotesk in 2025; by 2026 it is a tell.

The defence is not a better list. It is a **reason**. Every choice (face, colour, move, sound,
word) must trace to something true in the brand's world. "It looks premium" is not a reason.
"The red is the colour of the oven door in the bakery's first shop" is.

## 2. Provenance is the premium signal

The 2026 award winners all made provenance visible: Coinbase faked a game engine with painted
costumes in camera (Cannes Film Craft Grand Prix); Apple's "A Critter Carol" used imperfect
handmade puppets; Claude's Super Bowl films were crafted live action about the category's own
tension (Cannes Film Grand Prix). Audiences now read "it was really made" as quality.

In a free, code-drawn production, provenance has three honest sources:

1. **True pixels.** The real product, captured, doing a real task.
2. **True data.** Real, aggregated, anonymised numbers, never decorative ones.
3. **True rules.** A motion system derived from a real physical process the brand lives in
   (the rise and bake of a loaf, the swipe of a payment), so the behaviour itself is evidence.

Faking provenance (paper shaders standing in for craft, fake metadata chrome, invented
dashboards, mock billboards) is the 2026 version of a stock-photo smile. Creatives named it
"dishonesty" in April 2026.

## 3. The method

Each step has an output. Do not move on without it.

### 3.1 Interrogate the brief (output: the brief paragraph)

Answer these, from the request and the context, before anything else. Ask the person only the
ones you cannot answer and whose answer changes the film; state assumptions for the rest.

- **Who watches, where, and with sound on or off?** A muted autoplay hero, a deck slide, a
  Reel and a keynote screen are four different films.
- **What do they do after?** Book a demo, remember a name, trust a product. One action.
- **What is the crop and the length?** 16:9, 9:16, 4:5, a 1200px card. Length follows the
  surface, not ambition.
- **What is fixed?** Brand assets, product, legal, claims that can or cannot be made.
- **What is the deadline, really?** Scope the tier to it (`SKILL.md` section 1).

### 3.2 Gather specific material (output: the facts sheet)

James Webb Young (1939): an idea is a new combination of old elements, and the ability to
combine depends on how much specific material you hold. Collect **nouns, not adjectives**:

- What the product does, in the customer's words. The screens, the states, the numbers.
- The physical world the product lives in: materials, sounds, light, rituals, places, times
  of day. For a neighbourhood bakery: the starter in its jar, the deck oven, the four o'clock
  start, the scored loaf opening in the heat, flour on the bench, the queue at opening, the
  paper bag, the chalkboard prices.
- The people: operators, customers, what they complain about, what they love.
- The brand's assets: mark, palette, type, voice, any motion already approved.
- **The category's clichés**: list the ten images every competitor uses. For bakeries
  (judgement): flour thrown in slow motion, a loaf torn open with steam rising, golden-hour
  croissant close-ups, a baker dusting the bench, an overhead of loaves on a rack, a smiling
  baker in an apron, a wooden peel sliding into the oven. Any of them in your film needs a
  reason better than "it's a bakery".

Aim for twenty or more specific facts before the first idea.

### 3.3 Write the one truth (output: one sentence)

Tor Myhren's "nail theory" at Apple: one core idea per product film, everything else is a
layer, and "layers are killers". Write the one thing a customer would recognise as true. If it
needs "and", there are two films. Music is rarely the idea.

### 3.4 Find the mechanic (output: what the form does)

The idea is not a look. It is what the film's form does to enact the truth:

- a **camera grammar that changes** at the story's turn (Coinbase: fixed game angles become
  free cinematography as the hero breaks out);
- a **borrowed format** (Uncommon put beauty buzzwords into a periodic table);
- a **physical law** as the motion system (Collins built OFFF on magnetism);
- a **gesture vocabulary** (Pentagram's PayPal moves like tapping and swiping);
- a **structure** (one continuous take; a single day; a countdown; a loop that closes).

Test: describe the film with no style adjectives. If nothing is left, it is a style, not an idea.

### 3.5 Diverge, then kill (output: ten loglines, three developed, one chosen)

- Write **ten** one-line concepts. At least **three from outside the category** (what would a
  watchmaker, a weather forecast, a choir, a cartographer, a ship's log do with this truth?).
- **Kill the first idea**: it is the human version of distributional convergence. Kill the
  remake (redoing a famous ad) and anything a model would produce from a one-line prompt.
- Develop **three** properly: mechanic, structure in beats, the one frame, the sound idea, the
  risk. Apple's reported 10 to 3 to 1 practice is the model, even if parts of it are lore.
- Choose **one**, and write down why the other two lost.

Provocations when stuck (Oblique Strategies spirit): invert the brief; make the product
invisible; tell it in one shot; tell it backwards; what is the category embarrassed about;
what would this look like in 1972; remove the screen entirely; make the sound the picture.

### 3.6 Run the novelty tests (all must pass before production)

| Test | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| No adjectives | The logline survives with every style word removed |
| Swap | Cover the logo: a competitor could not run it unchanged |
| One-line prompt | A model could not produce it from one sentence |
| Forbids something | The motion system has a "never" list |
| Silhouette | Strip colour, type and logo: the behaviour alone is still recognisable |
| Thirty years | Ogilvy: could the rule still be in use in 2056? Trends cannot. |
| Category | None of the ten listed clichés appears without a stated reason |

### 3.7 Design the motion identity (output: rules plus a never list)

Every strong kinetic identity in the research does the same five things (DIA, Collins,
Studio Dumbar, Pentagram, Koto):

1. Pick **one physical or behavioural metaphor that is true of the brand** (a law, a gesture,
   a native behaviour, a signal), and test it against the one truth.
2. Write it as **rules**: what moves, what never moves, the easing signature, the timing range
   in frames, how it responds. Timing carries meaning; outside the range the meaning breaks
   (Collins on OFFF: "Move too fast, and the pull is lost; too slow and the energy fades").
3. Treat every still as **evidence of the system's performance** (Mitch Paone, DIA).
4. Build it as **a tool**: in this kit, a parametrised Remotion component in the film's folder.
5. Give it a **sound twin**: one motif that can be re-orchestrated (Mastercard's sonic brand),
   and sound behaviour that obeys the same envelope as the motion.

And write the **never list**. A system that forbids nothing is a mood board.

Worked example (judgement, for a bakery): the prove and the bake. Long patient rise, one
release when the oven door opens, a slow soft cooling. Never: bounce, overshoot, snap (dough
does not overshoot). Counter-move: the crack of the crust as the knife goes in, fast and
sharp, used rarely. Sound twin: filtered noise with the same envelope, the oven's breath.

### 3.8 Treatment (output: one page)

1. Logline (one sentence, no adjectives).
2. The one truth and the mechanic.
3. Structure: beats with timings on the beat grid.
4. **Colour script**: the colour and luminance arc across the film, act by act (Pixar's
   practice). If the ending is not brighter, darker or warmer than the opening, nothing happened.
5. Type behaviour (faces, sizes, the one signature move).
6. Motion rules and never list.
7. Sound: motif, palette, where the silence goes.
8. **The poster frame**: the single frame that would be the thumbnail and the share image.
   Design it first and build the film to arrive there.
9. The risk: what could go wrong. If nothing could, nobody will remember it.

### 3.9 Style frames (output: 3 to 5 rendered stills)

Render, do not describe. In the real engine, at full resolution: the opening frame, the poster
frame, a product frame, the end frame. Each must pass:

- **Pause anywhere**: it is a composed image, not a mid-transition smear.
- **Squint**: blur it; the focal point survives.
- **Thumbnail**: at 160px wide it still reads.
- **slop-lint** clean.

Show them to yourself next to two canon references at the same size before going further.

### 3.10 Animatic, then build, then the crit

Greyscale boxes on the real beat grid with a scratch pulse (`SKILL.md` section 2, step 7). Then
build the signature move as a motion test before the rest. Then run the crit (section 5) on
every cut.

## 4. Pushing back

A director pushes back on bad direction, then commits. The protocol:

1. **Name what they actually want.** "Make it look like Apple" means "make it feel premium and
   inevitable". "Add particles" means "make it feel like technology".
2. **Say which requested elements work against that, and why, specifically.** One reason per
   element, never a blanket "that's cliché".
3. **Offer the better route**, concretely enough to picture.
4. **If they still want theirs, make theirs and make it well.** Say the disagreement once, then
   commit. Never sulk, never relitigate, never sandbag the version you argued against.

| Request | Why it fails | Offer instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Make it look like an Apple ad" | Apple's look comes from one product truth per film and visible craft, not white space and kinetic captions. The clone look is its own cliché. | Find the one truth; spend the craft on the hero object, light and sound. |
| Purple/blue gradient, glow, particles "so it feels techy" | The exact centre of the distribution; audiences read it as AI-made. | One true data visual, or the product itself doing the thing. |
| Inter / Poppins / Space Grotesk | Defaults; they say nothing about the brand. | A face chosen for a reason in the brand's world (type-library.md). |
| Floating tilted 3D UI cards | The 2024 to 2026 SaaS mock-up cliché, and dishonest about the product. | The real UI, flat and true, on a locked camera. |
| AI voiceover "to save time" | Flat prosody, misread names; NIQ found AI ads leave weaker memories. | No voiceover with type and picture carrying it, or a real voice recorded on a phone. |
| "Epic trailer music" | Stock-feeling beds are the audio slop; risers into logos are the template. | A motif someone could hum, designed sound, and one silence before the reveal. |
| "Cinematic and epic" | A prompt adjective, not a direction. | Ask what in the film is true and specific; make that big. |
| "Animate everything" | Motion for its own sake is named among the most tired trends of 2026. | Decide what must move and why; most things hold. |
| "Mock up a billboard / fake dashboard" | Unless it will run or exists, it is the dishonesty named in 2026. | Show real placements or none. |
| "Put all the features in" | A feature list is a brochure; layers kill (Myhren). | One structure that reveals each feature as a consequence (a single day, a single guest's loop). |

## 5. The crit: ten questions before anyone else sees it

Ask these of every cut, in writing, with the frame numbers that answer them.

1. What is the idea in one sentence, with no style words?
2. Is it relevant to the brief and true to the product?
3. Could a competitor run it? (swap test)
4. What is the risk? If nothing could go wrong, nobody will remember it.
5. Where is the craft visible, frame by frame? Name the frames.
6. Which right-brain features does it have (a character, a place, a melody, a laugh, something
   unsaid)? Which left-brain features can go (words on screen, voiceover, a rhythmic bed)?
   Orlando Wood's System1 work links the first set to stronger long-term effects.
7. Which distinctive asset does it build, and will we use the same one next time? For a new
   brand, the first films are asset-building films: consistency across films beats novelty
   within one (Jenni Romaniuk).
8. What is the single peak moment, and is everything else quieter so it lands?
9. What did we make up? Fake data, fake billboard, fake testimonial: out.
10. Did it make me gasp, do I wish I had thought of it, could it run for thirty years? (Ogilvy)

Pixar's rule applies to all of it: early on, every film is bad. The job is to get from bad to
not bad by showing the ugly work early and fixing what the crit finds.

## 6. Composition and the director's eye

- **One focal point per frame.** Everything else is quieter in size, contrast, saturation or
  focus. The squint test finds the failures.
- **Scale contrast** reads as confidence: one very large thing and one very small thing beat
  five medium things.
- **Negative space is a decision**, not leftover. Asymmetric compositions with one dominant
  element beat centred stacks.
- **Motivated light.** Light comes from somewhere in the world (a stove, a window, a screen)
  with a direction, a colour temperature and a falloff. Uniform light is the CG tell.
- **The colour script carries emotion across the film**; the palette carries brand within a frame.
- **Faces and hands are the strongest focal pull in any frame.** In code-drawn work, do not
  attempt photoreal people; use silhouettes, shadows, hands as shapes, or leave people as the
  implied subject (the empty chair, the coat still on the hook).
- **Text is a frame element, not a caption.** Set it on the grid, in the composition, at a size
  and place chosen for the frame, with the camera locked while it is read.
- **Design for the crop it plays in.** A 9:16 cut is recomposed, never cropped from 16:9.
