# Concept

The thesis: **Hark turns sound into names.** The film's form should do the same thing in front of
the viewer, so the proof is in the mechanic rather than in a claim.

## Three directions

1. **The picture is the sound (chosen).** One continuous shot at dawn. The app's card shows what
   it hears as faint dots computed from the real soundtrack; when it names a bird, that bird's
   song is traced through the dots in its colour, and the bird lifts out of the card and flies to
   its branch. The full chorus pulls apart into six lanes. Today folds into a month of mornings.
   At the end the robin sings and its drawn song settles, note by note, into the word "hark".
   *Mechanic:* sound becomes a name, literally, on screen and in sync.
2. **The field guide.** A naturalist's plates fill in one by one as each bird is named, with
   pressed-paper texture and handwritten captions. Beautiful, but it shows a book, not an app,
   and the sound would be decoration rather than the subject.
3. **The phone in the hand.** A person at a window at dawn, the phone naming birds. Familiar
   territory for app launches; it needs people and a real phone, which code draws poorly, and
   the product would be a small rectangle in the middle of the frame.

Direction 1 is the only one where the film's own construction proves the claim: every mark on
the card is the soundtrack, every name lands on its bird's song, and the brand mark is made of
song. It is also the only one that could not be mistaken for another app's film.

## Risks named up front

- Synthesised birdsong can sound cheap. Keep each bird to its real shape and register, and let
  the room (air in the leaves, a quiet chord) carry the rest.
- A dark card in a dark pre-dawn wood can disappear. The sky warms as birds are named, so the
  contrast grows with the story.
- Six names in five seconds could be a blur. One name a second, each on a beat, each with its own
  colour, its own note and its own bird flying out.
